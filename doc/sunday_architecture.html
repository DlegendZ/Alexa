What Sunday is

Sunday is the program. The assistant has a name of its own.

Everything here calls it Sunday, and that is the package, the process, the entry points and %LOCALAPPDATA%\Sunday\. What it answers to is [assistant] name, and on this build that is Alexa — which also settles the wake phrase, since alexa is one of the three openWakeWord ships pretrained. Renaming the program would move the filed turns and buy nothing anybody sees; renaming the assistant is one line.

“The three strings a person meets” was the first count and it was low. It counted the places the name is printed — the system prompt, a rendered past exchange, the terminal banner — and missed the places it is spoken, which is a larger set the moment a 2b is doing the speaking. A tool result is a script: list_capabilities ends by telling the model to relay what it just read, and every sandbox refusal ends by telling it what to say. A name written into one of those is a name you hear. Sunday is not allowed into that folder, in a voice that answers to Alexa.

So the rule is stronger than “interpolate the configured name”. Those sentences are addressed to the assistant about itself, so they are written in the second person and carry no name at all: refused: that path is outside the folders you may open needs no configuration to stay true. The name appears once, in [assistant] name, and the two tests that hold this enumerate every refusal the sandbox can produce rather than the four that were once reported.

A desktop assistant you talk to. It hears a wake word, listens, answers out loud, and shows a glowing core that tells you what it is doing. You can also just type at it.

One small language model on your own machine does the thinking, reads your files, checks the weather and looks up prices. When it genuinely needs the open web, it goes through a single guarded door — and what passes through that door is written by something that has never seen your private data.

That last part is the whole design. Everything else follows from it.

The one sentence version

A local model can use your private things and the public web in the same breath, because the query that goes outside is composed in a room your private things were never allowed into.

Status — this is no longer only a plan

All thirteen milestones are built and running: the agent, the tools and the sandbox, the guardrail, the airlock and its web pipeline, memory, streaming, the sidecar's socket, the whole of voice — wake word, VAD and transcription in; Kokoro, three layers of echo control and barge-in out — and then the window itself. You say the phrase once and then talk, and there is now an orb to watch while you do. Calendar and mail came last and read-only, as planned. The shell builds, launches, spawns the sidecar, reads the handshake and drives a whole turn. Build order has the detail, including the one piece not yet produced — the installer.

This document has been revised against what the build found. Where a passage now reads differently from the original plan, it is because running the thing disagreed with it — those places say so rather than quietly reading as though they were always right. The largest are the splitter's non-break rule, the silence when the door is withdrawn, what retrieval hands back to the model, and one honest limit on the airlock's guarantee.

The two adapters are marked client because they are not graph nodes. The graph is exactly memory_read → agent → compose_reply → memory_write; the adapters sit outside it, in whichever client is driving — the terminal, later the sidecar. That is what Stage 01 already says in words: speech is converted before the graph and back after it. Keeping them out means one graph serves the text and voice clients with no modality branch inside it.

One thing a reader will look for in state and not find: the turn's message list. It lives in the runtime for the duration of one turn, alongside the notice list and the turn log. State is what the graph decides on, and a message list is not a decision.

Part one — the system

Stage 01

Voice in, voice out

Speech never enters the graph. The input adapter turns audio into a string and sets modality. The output adapter reads modality and either prints or speaks. The text client and the voice client share one memory, one agent, and one set of bugs.

Which transcriber, and why it is not the one the numbers picked

Four were measured on the same eight labelled clips, clean and with noise added. moonshine base: 0.0% word error, 123 ms a clip, and still 0.0% at 8 dB. parakeet tdt 0.6b int8: 2.6% and 704 ms. whisper large-v3-turbo int8: 0.0% and 5414 ms. whisper small.en int8: 2.6% and 1298 ms. On the clip the pipeline actually builds, Moonshine returns “What’s the weather in Jakarta?” in 102 ms and Parakeet returns “Surface what’s the weather in Jakarta?” in 238 — rendering the tail of the wake phrase that the pre-roll deliberately keeps.

Parakeet ships anyway, and the reason is what the table cannot see. Those clips are one synthetic voice, clean, no accent and no room; a set the incumbent scores zero on cannot separate anything, it can only fail a challenger. On the one recording of a real person in a real room, Parakeet was the only one of the four to get the wake phrase nearly right. It is 600M parameters against Moonshine base’s 61M and sits at the top of the open ASR leaderboard, where Moonshine does not appear at all.

So the trade is about 130 ms a turn against headroom on speech the test set does not contain, and it is a bet rather than a result. [models] stt names either, only the named one is downloaded, and both stay in the tree — a switch you cannot flip is not a record of a decision.

Four graphs, and one library with an excuse

The wake word and the VAD run directly on onnxruntime, which is already here for the embeddings, because their packages cost more than the models do: openWakeWord brings scikit-learn and a TensorFlow-Lite runtime to do three sequential session.run calls, and Silero ships a 2 MB graph behind torch.

Kokoro and Parakeet keep theirs, and the excuse is the same one twice. What kokoro-onnx supplies is espeak-ng and a pronunciation dictionary; what onnx-asr supplies is a mel front-end exported as its own graph and a decode loop that emits a token and a duration together and skips frames accordingly. Both are specific algorithms rather than glue, and neither brings anything heavy — unlike NeMo, which is the supported way to run Parakeet and would bring torch.

Every session goes through one function, including the two a library builds. onnxruntime’s intra-op pool busy-waits between operators by default, and the thing that loses that contest is the audio callback — it drops microphone frames and reports input overflow, in the middle of a reply. Turning spinning off is measurably faster besides: 867 ms against 968 for the same Kokoro sentence, because the pool stops fighting itself. The first attempt set it only on the graphs this package constructs, which missed the synthesiser and the encoder — the two largest, and therefore the only two capable of causing the problem.

The trade is that the tensor shapes become your problem, and two are not guessable from outside. Silero needs the 64 samples before its 512-sample window handed back as context; leave them out and it does not raise, it returns 0.02 for clear speech, which reads as a broken microphone. The merged Moonshine decoder wants a zero-length dummy past on the first pass, and returns its cross-attention cache empty on every pass after — feed that back and the batch dimension collapses to zero on the second token.

Why VAD is its own component

Neither transcriber streams. Both take a clip you hand them, so something has to decide where that clip begins and ends. A streaming STT would know on its own; these buy their size and their accuracy by not knowing, and you supply the ears instead.

And it wants a clip shaped like the ones it was trained on. Padded with silence, it emits end-of-sequence as its first token and returns an empty string — measured here at two seconds of padding either side of four seconds of clear speech. Every clip this pipeline builds is padded by construction, half a second of pre-roll in front and seven-tenths of trailing silence behind, so the clip is trimmed to the voiced span plus 250 ms before it is handed over. Deciding where the clip ends was never the whole job; deciding what to cut off it is the other half.

The consequence: the transcriber waits for you to stop talking before it writes anything — neither of the two streams. For short commands that is invisible. For a twenty-second ramble you wait for the whole utterance first.

Barge-in

VAD runs continuously for the whole turn — not only while Kokoro is speaking. The moment it hears real speech, three things happen at once: playback stops, the TTS queue is flushed, and the model stream is cancelled. The partial turn is discarded and never written to memory.

The thinking counts. The gap between your question and the first word of the answer is seconds long — memory, then tools, then generation — and it is the likeliest moment to change your mind. Nothing was watching it: the wake word is only consulted when idle, and there was no reply to talk over. Speaking, thinking, and the tail after speaking are one rule rather than three cases — a turn is happening, and talking during any of it interrupts. Only a genuinely idle listener needs the phrase.

This is the most latency-sensitive moment in the system. It is instant because VAD is always listening and the transcriber is always resident, so nothing has to wake up. See Resources for why everything stays loaded, and Echo control for how it avoids hearing itself.

Streaming

Kokoro says sentence one while the agent is still writing sentence two, and the text client prints tokens the moment they arrive. You wait for one sentence, not for the whole reply.

Splitter rules

Splitting on every . mispronounces every decimal and every abbreviation. Four rules, in this order:

  1. Split on . ? ! — but only when whitespace or the end of the stream follows.
  2. Never split inside a known non-break: a dot between digits, a known abbreviation before the dot, a single initial, a trailing ellipsis.
  3. Force a split at the last comma at or before 180 characters, so one long unpunctuated sentence still starts playing.
  4. Send whatever is left when the stream ends.

The first sentence is allowed to be very short on purpose. A two-word opener reaching Kokoro straight away is the whole speed win.

Two corrections from building it

Rule 2 first said a short capitalised word before the dot. That is a tempting heuristic and it is wrong: it also matches “Sure.” and “Yes.” and “Done.” — exactly the short openers rule 1 exists to release early. The heuristic and the goal were in direct conflict, so the non-breaks are now an explicit list — Mr, Dr, Fig, No, e.g and the rest — plus the single-initial case in “J. Smith”.

Rule 3 first said a comma once the buffer passes 180 characters, which reads as: start looking at 180. A sentence whose only comma sits at 140 then never splits at all — the case the rule exists for. It looks backwards from the limit instead, and only falls forward when there is no comma inside it.

The one stream, two sinks

The part streaming cannot fix

Streaming only removes the waiting it can see. The gap between you finishing and the first token is transcription plus tool time. That gap needs a visible state on the orb, or it looks frozen. In voice it is silence — never a spoken “let me check that for you”.

  • Thinking blocks never reach either sink. When thinking is on, the reasoning comes back separately and the filter drops it before the fork.
  • memory_write stays off the critical path. It runs after the last token, so a disk write cannot delay the first word.
  • A cancelled or broken turn is not saved. Partial replies never reach Chroma.
Stage 02

Memory read

Memory is looked up before anything else happens, including before the agent decides what to do. Otherwise the one component that needs to know what “there” or “that file” refers to is the one running without it.

Short-term memory sits in RAM and lives as long as the session. Long-term memory is Chroma on disk. They are not alternatives — every turn reads both.

An exchange records the paths its tools were called with, not only what was said. That sounds like bookkeeping and is the whole point of the tier: short-term memory exists so “that file” and “there” resolve, and for a while it could not do it. “Move it back to documents” needs the path the last turn moved, and the last reply was “the file has been moved to the work folder” — no path in it anywhere. The model filled the gap with a path from two turns earlier, which was the one place the file was no longer at.

Ranking has no idea of “nothing”

Vector search does not answer “is anything relevant?”. It answers “which are closest?” — and something is always closest, even when everything is far away. So retrieval takes the top k and then throws away anything past a distance cutoff. Returning zero documents has to be a normal outcome, or every quiet turn fills the prompt with noise.

The collection is opened in cosine space, not Chroma's default squared L2, so the 0.45 in config means what a reader assumes it means: 0 identical, 1 unrelated. On the default metric the same number is a much stricter and far less interpretable threshold.

Each stored document carries ts, session_id, tools_used and provenance. That last field matters: retrieved documents inherit whatever provenance the original turn had, so a memory of a private turn stays private. The airlock never sees retrieved memory at all — see Stage 05.

A document already sitting in the recent block is dropped rather than shown twice. The same turn is in both tiers by design, so without this the last few turns of the current session appear in the window two times over.

What a recalled turn hands over

Storage keeps the whole turn. Retrieval does not hand the whole turn to the model. A recalled turn is rendered as the user's line plus the tools that ran — (answered using: get_asset_price) — and Sunday's own past reply is left out of it. A recalled session summary still renders whole, because the fold prompt already wrote it as third-person notes rather than as a reply to copy. Turns in the current session's recent block are untouched: conversational continuity needs them.

Why — a hallucination that compounds

This was measured, not guessed, and it is the largest correction the build made to this document.

Asked for the silver price against an empty store, the reply was one correct sentence. Asked again with that turn in memory, the reply repeated the first one's invented trend — “it has been climbing steadily on recent trading days” — with a fresh price pasted in. A 2b treats its own retrieved prose as the template for the new answer. So a decoration written once is stored, recalled as though it were a fact, and re-served every time the question is asked.

Three prompt phrasings failed to stop it. The one finally strong enough made the model distrust the record wholesale and refuse a fact the user had plainly stated. Rendering is the only layer where this is fixable, because it is the only layer that decides what the model gets to read.

The cost is real and worth stating: a fact that exists only in Sunday's past reply, and nowhere in the user's words or a re-runnable tool, is no longer recalled. That is the right trade. What the user told you is in their lines; what a tool told you can be fetched again.

Two consequences follow. A recalled turn now renders as its question, so asking the same thing twice would retrieve a line repeating the question and carrying no fact — those are dropped. And the block needs framing when it reaches the model: which lines are the user, that facts they stated still hold, and that the user is to be spoken about in the second person. Without that last part the 2b answers “My landlord is Pak Yusuf”.

The window, split five ways

Memory is not the only claimant. Tool results land in the window, and so does thinking when it is switched on. Give each a fixed allowance and overflow becomes arithmetic you can check instead of a silent truncation.

The window is 23552, and the number belongs to the card rather than to the design. It was 8192 for most of this build — chosen before anything was measured and never revisited — then 32768 once it was, because on the 2b reserved KV cache that never gets filled is nearly free: 3286 MiB and 63.5 tokens a second at 8192 against 3546 MiB and 63.1 at 32768.

And it is a cliff, not a slope. One step further up the 1024 grid, at 24576, 15% of the model is on the processor and 40.1 tokens a second replaces 46.1. The far end of that curve was measured too, because it was asked for: 70000 puts 40% on the processor and costs 63% of the speed — 16.8 tokens a second against 46.1. The binding constraint is 6 GB of VRAM, and no amount of free disk changes it.

It has been 16384, then 20480, then this, measured the same way each time and moving because there was more or less on the card. The ceiling is not a property of the model. It is the model, the window and whatever else wants the card at that moment, which makes it a number to re-measure rather than one to reason about. OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE=q8_0 is the next lever and would roughly halve the KV; on this arithmetic it puts 32768 back inside the card, at the cost of an environment variable and an Ollama restart.

The arithmetic runs on an estimate — 3.6 characters to the token — not a real tokeniser. Every accurate option downloads a vocabulary on first use, and an assistant whose whole premise is that it works offline should not put a network call in its context accounting. The estimate errs high, so the budget is conservative rather than optimistic. If it ever turns out to matter, Ollama can be asked to count.

The five slices are sized against what is free, not against the window

They add up to 19456, and as a statement of ratios that is what they are. But they are not the only claimants. The system prompt (276 tokens), the bound tool schemas (1552), the memory framing block, the standing system lines — which folders are open, whether the web door is shut — and the loop's own two nudges cost around 2550 tokens that no slice pays for, and the reply needs room of its own.

So the fixed overhead and the reply come off the top and the five slices are scaled into what remains, keeping the ratios the config asked for. Size them to the window instead and num_ctx overruns, and Ollama answers that by dropping the oldest messages without telling anyone — the silent truncation this whole stage exists to prevent.

There is nothing to scale, and that is deliberate rather than lucky: 19456 of slices plus 3584 of overhead and reply comes to 23040 of the 23552, and the rest is slack for a large tool result. The scaling is very good at hiding the fact that it fired. These slices read 2048/6144/2048/4096 at the old window and were used whole; carrying them into 16384 untouched would have quietly shrunk every one by 0.81, and nothing anywhere would have said so. A configured number that is not the number in use is the same failure as a number nobody rechecks. The scaling stays, because it is what makes a smaller window degrade rather than break on the day this runs somewhere with less to spare.

overhead_tokens is a measurement, and a test measures it: add a tool or a system line without moving the number and the suite fails rather than the window quietly shrinking. It has moved four times — 1200, then 1750 for delete_file, list_capabilities and the roots line, 2000 for copy_file and move_file, 2400 for a system prompt that actually routes them, 2600 for calendar_read and mail_search, and 2816 when the assistant was given a character — the system prompt went from 276 tokens to 371 and the loop gained a second nudge. Personality is not free, and that is where it is paid for. The 2600 move is what the test is for: nothing had broken, the suite was green, and the slack under 2400 had quietly fallen to 102 tokens.

At 8192 that was 29% of everything and the slices were being scaled to 0.61 to fit under it: the recent-turns allowance was 1884 tokens where the config said 3072, and nothing anywhere said so. At 23552 the 2816 is 12%, and the slices are sized to fit rather than scaled into it. Twelve tools costing 1552 tokens of bound schema is a cost worth knowing; it was not a reason not to add the eleventh and twelfth, which are the calendar and the mailbox.

Why the slices are doubled and not tripled

Widening the window did not widen memory: the five slices still summed to 8192 inside 32768, and 21408 tokens sat unused. Doubling them was the obvious move and tripling was the tempting one, so the difference was measured rather than argued. Moving to the 4b took the window back to 16384 and the slices came down with it — explicitly, to numbers that fit, rather than by letting the scaling do it quietly.

Prompt processing on this card runs at about 0.15 ms per token above 3k — 331 ms at 1349 tokens, 1031 ms at 6629, 2002 ms at 13229. A turn pays that before it says a word. At 8192 of slices the worst case was around 1.6 s; at 19456 it is about 3.3 s; much past that it is over four seconds on every turn of a long session, which is the wrong trade for something meant to be spoken to. That, and not the card, is what stops the slices simply being grown to fill whatever window fits.

What makes this affordable at all is that slices are allowances, not usage. A short session fills none of them and pays nothing, so the cost arrives gradually and only in the sessions long enough to have earned it. The ratios the config asked for are unchanged; every slice simply doubled.

A number no test can check is a number nobody rechecks

8192 survived a whole build, two code reviews and a dozen sessions of tuning. Every test passed the entire time, and they were the right tests: they asserted the slices fit under the configured window, which they always did — because the scaling made them fit. Nothing asserted the window was the right size, because nothing could. That is a measurement of a graphics card, not an invariant of the code.

It came out when the claim “the token budget is a constraint, not a bug” was questioned rather than accepted, and measuring it took four minutes. The transferable part is not about context windows. It is that the numbers a suite cannot check are exactly the ones that go stale, so they have to be re-measured deliberately — not when something finally feels wrong.

Stage 03

The agent

One model, qwen3.5:4b, served by Ollama. It talks to you, decides which tools to call, runs them, and writes the reply. There is no separate classifier, because tool selection is the routing decision — done by machinery the model is already trained for.

The system prompt is short because a long one comes back out of its mouth

Seven lines: no markdown; say only what a tool returned, and never claim you did something a tool did not do; moving a file is move_file; a folder as the destination; do not go looking to check whether you are allowed; chat is not a job; and do not explain your own rules unless that is the question. Each was earned by a transcript where the opposite happened — the last one by the recital quoted below.

The no-markdown line is the one that could not hold on its own. The model obeys it most of the time, and most of the time is not good enough for text that gets read aloud, where a backtick is pronounced and a dash at the start of a line is a spoken word. So the same rule is applied again where it cannot be forgotten: the token stream is stripped inside stream.fork, before either sink and before memory, so what is shown, what is spoken and what is filed are the same characters. Asterisks, backticks, and a list marker at the start of a line — nothing else. Underscores are left alone, because half the filenames on the machine have one.

It was twice that length for a while, and every extra rule was also true. Then the model started reciting it: “The user said they love you sunday. You are Sunday, a local personal assistant on their Windows computer. This statement is an expression of affection rather than a request. Do not call any tools to reply.” That is the constitution being read aloud to the person it was written about. A 2b spends its attention on the last thing it read, and a long enough prompt is the last thing it read. 276 tokens, and every line has to earn its length.

Why a small Qwen and not a bigger something else

Tool calling is the one capability this whole design rests on, and Qwen is trained for agentic use in a way most small models are not. The size within that family was the open question, and the argument for starting small was that a 2b which reliably picks the right tool beats a 4b that fumbles the arguments.

It was the right way round and the wrong conclusion. Measured on the same turns, the 4b picks the same tools with the same arguments and writes markedly better sentences — so the trade the 2b was accepted for was never collected. What it costs is 46.7 tokens a second against 64.8, and about a second a turn. See the end of this stage for what that bought.

Thinking is a dial, and you hold it

Thinking is available but off by default, and the model does not get to choose. Models over-use reasoning when allowed to opt in, and on a 2b running locally the cost is real: hundreds of tokens before the first useful word, and those tokens eat the same window everything else is sharing.

The trigger is deterministic, in code:

  • Three or more tool results to reconcile
  • The guardrail blocked something and the gap must be explained
  • The tool-call cap was reached

Everything else runs with thinking off, inside the 2048-token headroom from Stage 02. That headroom is a reservation, not an instruction: Ollama's API takes think: true | false and has no token budget to pass, so thinking_budget is enforced by the slice accounting alone and never reaches the model.

Tool rounds do not write prose

A round inside the tool loop exists to answer one question: is there a tool call in this response? Whatever prose comes with it is discarded, because compose_reply writes the reply you actually see. So those rounds are capped at 384 predicted tokens.

Without the cap, a chitchat turn generates a whole reply twice — once thrown away in the loop, once streamed to you. The cap turns that into a fraction of a second.

The cost you are accepting

A 2b writes thinner prose than a bigger model. Tool selection should be better; the sentences themselves will read flatter, especially when combining several results. If it disappoints, the fix is a larger single model — not a second one, and not a cloud one. This is what happened, and the rest of this block is the record of it.

Measured, once it was running. Tool selection held up. Two prose habits survived every prompt fix tried. It slips person, answering “My landlord is Pak Yusuf” for a fact the user stated, roughly one time in two. And it decorates: a wind direction the weather tool never returned, a note that silver spikes during geopolitical tension. Tightening the prompt to “report what the tool returned and stop there” reduced the second habit without ending it.

Neither is a privacy failure, and neither survives into the airlock. Both were exactly the trade named above.

Settled by moving to qwen3.5:4b. The same three questions, same tools, same arguments, and the difference is in the sentences. The 2b answered the gold price with the price and then “I have provided the information directly here without…”, narrating its own conduct; the 4b answered “The current gold price is 4,367.50 USD per ounce.” and stopped. Asked the weather, the 2b wrote 26.5°C — a degree sign, in text that gets spoken — where the 4b wrote “26.5 degrees Celsius”. It cost a second a turn and 768 MiB of card, and it did not cost tool accuracy, which was the thing the 2b was chosen for.

One habit did not improve and is worth naming: asked what it can do, the 4b paraphrases list_capabilities rather than reading it back, and dropped the file tools while doing so. The fast path hands it correct prose and it still edits it. qwen3:8b is pulled and would need a smaller window again; that comparison is not made yet.

Creating a folder was never asked for in any of those turns. A copy launders a credential, so the source is what gets refused

The obvious worry about a copy is the destination: it is how a file would leave the sandbox. That one is easy — the destination is resolved and checked for containment like every other path, and the sandbox is doing its ordinary job.

The source is the one that is easy to miss. The deny overlay works on paths: reading .env marks the turn secret and bolts the web door. Copy .env to notes.txt and the same bytes now sit at a path the overlay says nothing about — the next read is an ordinary private read, the turn stays clean, and the door stays open. Taint cannot save this, because by the time anything is read the credential is at a path that was never on the list.

So a credential source is refused, not tainted. It is the one place in this design where the answer is "no" rather than "yes, and the door shuts behind you".

The token file is on the credential list

google_token.json holds a refresh token. It sits in the deny overlay, so if the agent ever reads it the turn is marked secret and the door closes. The same is true of .env, which holds the deepseek key.

A fast path never fires on a compound message

The result is handed to the agent as an ordinary tool result, so the turn carries on normally — and for a while that was taken to mean a mixed question lost nothing. It lost half of itself, every time. “What is the price of gold and what is the weather in Jakarta” fired the weather pattern in code, and the model, seeing a tool result already sitting in the transcript, read the turn as finished and answered about the weather alone. It then added “no specific price was returned for this request”, which is true and useless: nothing had asked for one.

Telling the model was tried first — a system line saying the shortcut covers one clause and the rest still needs doing. The second half was still dropped. So a message containing a second instruction gets no shortcut at all: the fast path is insurance for the single-clause case where a fumbled argument would be obvious, and on a compound the model does the work, which it does correctly once nothing has answered ahead of it.

The same fix surfaced a second bug of a kind this document has already recorded once. _TAIL strips the words a person adds after a city name, and its and branch had no word boundary — so weather in Thailand asked for the weather in Thail. Stage 05 learned that lesson about sk- matching inside “ta|sk-oriented”. Any pattern that cuts a string has to say where a word begins.

Never means all three, and for a while it meant two of them. The compound test was written after the capability pattern rather than before it, so “what can you do and what is the weather in Jakarta” still took the shortcut — and the capability list is the worst of the three to answer half a question with. It is long, it is the last thing the model read, and a 2b reading it decides the turn was about itself. The turn came back as a tour of the tool belt with no weather in it. The test now runs before any pattern is tried, which is the only arrangement in which the sentence at the top of this block is true.

Overwriting asks. Creating does not. Deleting always does.

Reading is auto-execute; the sandbox is the whole check. Writing splits in two, because the two cases are not alike. Creating a file loses nothing, so it goes through silently. Replacing a file you already have is irreversible, so it stops and asks — naming the resolved path and the size of what is about to go.

The split is the point. A confirmation on every new note is a confirmation you learn to approve without reading, and by the time one actually matters it has stopped being a question. Asking rarely is what keeps the asking worth something.

A copy or a move asks about where it lands. The source survives a copy and is one call away from coming back after a move; what cannot be undone is whatever was already sitting at the destination. Same rule, applied to the end that loses something.

Deleting does not get the same split, because it has no half that is safe. There is no arrangement of the arguments where delete_file does not destroy something, so it asks every time — and the question says so, in those words: Delete C:\…\scratch.txt (17 bytes)? This cannot be undone. The only calls that skip it are the ones the sandbox or the deny overlay was going to refuse anyway.

A sink, not a graph interrupt

The obvious implementation is a LangGraph interrupt: pause the graph, save state, resume on the next message. That suspends and re-invokes the whole graph, so the turn's message list, its tool results and its streaming sinks all have to survive a checkpoint round trip — a great deal of machinery for one yes.

Instead the confirmation is a blocking callback handed to the turn, called in the tool loop immediately before write_file or delete_file, alongside the door checks. Same guarantee — nothing is written or destroyed until a person says so — and it works identically for both clients: the terminal prompts on stdin, the sidecar sends confirm and waits for confirm_response. The request carries an action, so a UI can render overwrite and delete differently rather than showing one generic card.

It fails closed. A session with no one attached cannot consent, so the write is refused rather than allowed. Silence is not a yes. And the sandbox still answers first: a path outside the roots, or a credential file, is refused before you are asked anything at all. Saying yes widens what you permit, never what the sandbox permits.

A cancelled turn releases any question still waiting, refusing it, so barge-in cannot leave a write parked behind a timeout.

A turn that called nothing is asked once more — and the asking never reaches the reply

The loop can see a tool fail. It cannot see the model decline to act, and that is the failure that actually happens: asked to move a file, a 2b either claims the move happened with no tool call anywhere in the turn, or refuses with reasoning it invented on the spot. No call ran, so nothing errored, so nothing prompts a retry.

So a round that produces no tool calls at all gets one more attempt, with the request restated. Once, and only when nothing ran: a turn that already used a tool has evidence to write from, and nudging there is how a small model talks itself into calling the same thing twice.

Both nudges are then removed before the reply is written. They are instructions to the loop, they sat last in the message list, and a 2b copies whatever wording is nearest — so it began answering with them: “I cannot use tools in this session”, “Do not call any tools to reply. Only respond naturally.” Sentences nobody wrote, delivered to the user as Sunday's own words. Facts about the turn stay, because the reply has to explain them. Instructions about how to think go.

Fast paths

Three deterministic patterns run in code before the model is asked anything — but only when the message carries a single instruction. See below.

  • what can you do / list your tools / apa saja kemampuan kamu → call list_capabilities
  • weather (in|for|at) <place> → call get_weather directly with the captured group
  • (price|how much) (of|is) <asset> → map the word to a symbol, call get_asset_price

These are not an optimisation. With a 2b and no larger model behind it, they are the insurance policy for the two most common requests — the ones where a fumbled argument would be most obvious.

A match does not short-circuit the turn. The answer is appended as an ordinary tool result and the loop runs as usual, so the model can still call more tools and a question with two halves loses neither.

Stage 05

The airlock

There is exactly one way for anything to leave this machine: ask_external. One function, one place to audit.

The problem it solves is the one that makes mixed questions hard. Consider:

The question this stage exists for

“What is the price of gold right now, and what did I write in my gold.txt about when I am going to sell it?”

Two halves. One is public and one is yours. A naive agent reads gold.txt, then writes a web query while holding your notes in its head — and your sell target ends up in a search box.

The fix is not to trust the model to be careful. It is to make sure the thing writing the outgoing query has never seen the private half.

How it works

When the agent wants the web, it does not hand over a finished query. It states an intent — ask_external("current analyst view on gold") — and that intent is treated as a hint, not as the query.

The airlock then runs a separate model call with a fresh context. That context contains the user's own words for this turn, any public results already gathered, and the intent hint. It contains no conversation history, no retrieved memory, and no private or secret tool results. Its only job is to emit one search query.

Why mixed questions are now safe

The two halves are answered separately and combined locally. The web half never receives the private half; the private half never leaves. The gold question needs no external call at all — the price is a local tool — but even when a genuine web search is needed, the same split holds.

Where the guarantee is thinner than it sounds

Everything above is built and holds, with one exception that this document should name rather than let a reader discover.

The hint may reorder what is already crossing. It may not add to it.

The fresh context excludes private results structurally — they are never assembled into the prompt, so there is nothing there to filter. But the intent hint is not a fourth kind of input; it is a model output. ask_external("current analyst view on gold") is written by the agent, which by then may have your gold.txt in scope. For a while nothing in the mechanism stopped a model from putting your sell target into its own intent string and handing it across — so “the query writer cannot leak” was true while “the thing that writes the hint cannot leak” was only a hope.

The hint is now vetted against exactly the material the prompt is about to contain: your line for this turn, and the public results. Every word of it has to already appear there. Selecting and reordering words that were going to cross anyway is the whole job of a hint, and it still does that. Introducing a word that was nowhere in the cleared material is the only thing it could do that would leak, and it is the only thing that was taken away.

gold price against my sell target of 4600 reaches the composer as gold price.

And the cleared material is your line for this turn plus your own last three. With only the current line, a question that points at something has nothing to point at. From a live session: “Check the internet for that.” The agent knew what “that” was and put it in the hint; the hint was vetted against one sentence containing no nouns and correctly stripped to nothing. The only text that left the machine was price of, and the reply spent a paragraph explaining that the search had found nothing about Bitcoin. The rule was working exactly as designed, so what had to change was the material rather than the check.

Your earlier lines are the same kind of thing as your current one — words you typed or said, carrying the user label that has always been allowed through. What is deliberately not included is the rest of the session: a reply may quote a file, a retrieved turn may be private, and a tool result has its own label and its own gate. Three lines, because the thing “that” refers to is the last thing said and never four turns back, and because every line here is a line that can reach the composer. The same turn afterwards sent current bitcoin price 2026.

Two details decide whether that holds. A number is one token, so 4,600 is not split into two innocent-looking fragments. And the filler list — the handful of words like the, current, price allowed through unconditionally so the hint still reads as English — is tiny and closed, because every word added to it is a hole. How many words were dropped comes back with the query and is traced: a silent filter is how you end up believing a guarantee you no longer have.

The guardrail

The airlock handles what the query says. The guardrail handles what a credential looks like, and it is deliberately narrow.

The threat model, stated plainly

Credentials must never leave. Topical context may. If a company name reaches a search engine, that is accepted — it is the same as typing it into a chat box yourself. If an API key reaches a search engine, that is a failure.

Where it lives

One function in the tool loop, which already sees both the arguments and the result:

for call in response.tool_calls:

Binding alone is not enough

A model can emit several tool calls in one response, so read_file(".ssh/config") and ask_external(…) can arrive together, decided before either ran. At bind time the turn was clean, so the door was open. That is why the check runs per call, in order — unbinding protects the next model invocation, the in-loop check protects the current one. You need both, and the second is the one people forget.

A prefix is not a shape

The list above is written as prefixes, and a prefix on its own is not specific enough to be safe. Two corrections, both found by running it over ordinary prose rather than over sample tokens:

Every shape is anchored to the start of a word. Without that anchor a prefix matches inside one: sk- turned “a ta|sk-oriented approach” into “a ta[redacted] approach”, and did the same to “di|sk-image-backup”. Ordinary English, blanked, with the credential notice fired over it.

And a prefix that is also a word needs its full shape. ASIA is English where AKIA is not, so an Asia-Pacific revenue report redacted; hf_ collides with snake_case, so hf_dataset_loader.py redacted. Both are fixed-length credentials, so spelling out the exact tail costs nothing and ends the ambiguity.

This is the false-positive tax this stage rejects entropy scoring to avoid, arriving through the front door instead. A shape is only worth adding if it can be tested against prose that must survive it — a passing test over a sample token proves nothing.

Deliberately not covered: a password you type into the chat yourself; a secret in a normally-named file such as notes.txt; an OTP inside an email. Catching those needs entropy scoring and Luhn checks, which block git hashes, UUIDs, order numbers and invoice references — a daily false-positive tax for a guarantee that still misses a password like budi1990. This is a chosen line, not an oversight.

On a shape match the guardrail redacts and continues rather than bouncing the query back. A rewrite loop makes the model rephrase around a problem it cannot see. You are told once — and by two different sentences, because there are two different things that can happen:

  • A query was scrubbed on its way out: “I removed something that looked like a credential before searching.”
  • A tool result was scrubbed on its way in: “Something in what I read looked like a credential, so I stripped it before reading the rest.”

One sentence covered both at first, and a .env read duly told the user a credential had been removed “before searching” when nothing had been searched. A privacy notice that misdescribes what happened is worse than no notice: it teaches you to discount the next one.

Shutting the door quietly is the same as not shutting it

Unbinding is invisible from the outside

Asked to read .env and then search the web, the first build did exactly what this document specifies: tainted the turn, withdrew ask_external for the next round, and answered. Confidently. With no search, and no mention anywhere that the search had not happened.

Nothing was wrong with the mechanism. The problem is that withdrawing a tool tells the model nothing — the tool is simply absent, and an absent tool leaves no trace to explain. Two additions, both required:

  • the model is handed one system line saying the tool was withdrawn and why, and to say plainly if you had asked for a lookup;
  • you get the notice whenever a turn goes tainted, not only when a call was actually refused — because in the common case there is no refusal to report, just a silence.

Whenever has an edge, and the edge was leaking. A turn that touched a credential and then lost the model — Ollama restarted, the stream cut — returned down the error path, which was written before any of this and returned before the notices were said. The door had shut, a key had been stripped, and the only thing you were told was that Ollama was unreachable. A broken turn still owes you an account of what it already did, so the notices are emitted there too, before done and while a sink still exists to hear them. A cancelled turn is deliberately not the same case: you stopped it, it is discarded, and apologising for work you stopped caring about is noise.

Several jobs in one message

The loop does not end when a tool returns. That sequencing is the point: the file gets the number the price tool returned rather than a memory of it.

A single round may also declare several calls at once, and each gets its own answer. Two independent questions — a price and a weather reading — come back as two calls in one round.

Eight calls is what makes both shapes affordable. Five was sized for a turn that meant one lookup, and a turn that picks the wrong tool, reads the refusal and corrects itself needs room for the recovery as well as the mistake.

A repeated failing call is refused without running

Asked to copy a file into a folder that did not exist, the model sent the identical move_file on rounds 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7 — spending the whole cap on one call that could never work, and ending the turn with an empty reply. RETRY_HINT says do not send the same call again unchanged in as many words. It sent it again unchanged.

A prompt cannot enforce a loop bound. The cap did stop it eventually, which is the cap doing its job badly: it is a backstop against runaway cost, not a way to notice the model is stuck. So the loop remembers the arguments of every call that failed, refuses an exact repeat without dispatching it, and ends the turn on a third.

Only failed calls are guarded. A repeated success is wasteful rather than pathological — and two of the guarantees on this page, the hop cap and the no-room refusal, are tested by issuing the same successful call twice. Guarding those broke both tests, which is how the distinction was found.

Eight rather than five. Five was sized for a turn that meant one lookup; a turn that picks the wrong tool, reads the refusal and corrects itself needs room for the recovery as well as the mistake. The transcript that moved it spent all five on reads and folder listings without ever reaching the tool that did the job.

Stage 08

The socket

The sidecar binds to 127.0.0.1 on an ephemeral port, writes {port, token} to %LOCALAPPDATA%\Sunday\handshake.json, and the shell reads it. Binding to loopback only, plus a per-launch bearer token, means nothing else on the machine can drive your assistant.

The shell reads it, and the WebView never does. Rust opens the file and hands the page two values through a command; a WebView that could read handshake.json could read everything beside it, which is the Chroma store and the Google token. The same two values come off the query string when the window is run in an ordinary browser, which is how it was built — a reload is a tenth of a second and a Rust build is not.

One trap, and it is the sort that fails intermittently and blames the network. The handshake is rewritten each launch, but the previous one is on disk until then, so a shell that reads the file straight after spawning finds the old port and connects to nothing — or to something else. It only counts once its started is no older than the spawn.

And query carries the exact text that left the machine — see Open decisions, where showing it was still an open question; having built it, the answer is that a debug client should always have it and the shipped UI can decide separately.

One turn runs at a time. A second text_input arriving mid-turn is answered with a notice rather than queued or run alongside — there is one model and one GPU, and pretending otherwise only moves the failure.

The terminal client has the same problem in a smaller shape, and it is worth stating because the fix is the general one. There is one stdin and two things want the lines on it: the turn loop, which takes questions, and a confirmation, which has to be answered mid-turn. Reading it from both is two threads racing for one descriptor — type y at an overwrite prompt and the turn loop is as likely to take it, answer it as a question, and leave the confirmation waiting for a line that has already been consumed. A file descriptor has one reader; if two parts of the program want what arrives on it, the reader routes rather than forks.

confirm is the only message on this socket that expects an answer. The turn's worker thread blocks on it, every attached client is asked, and the first reply wins. Nothing answers within two minutes and it resolves itself — to no, because a question nobody answered is not consent. Its action is overwrite or delete, so a UI can say which rather than showing one generic card.

trace is the backstage channel, and it is the one message on this socket that reports what is happening rather than what came back. See below.

error is the only message that can arrive before ready: a bad token gets one and then a close. After that it means the model became unreachable mid-turn, and it is always followed by done with committed:false.

Every line on this list is implemented. level was the last one waiting on a producer and now has one: the listener emits it ten times a second while the microphone is open, which is what lets a client show that the microphone is genuinely bound. A state that says listening over a flat meter is a device that was never opened.

It carries two numbers rather than one. rms is what the microphone hears; out is what is coming out of the speaker, at the microphone's rate, the same frame. The second was already being measured — it is the barge-in floor, and computing it is the only way to tell your voice from Sunday's — and it was being thrown away. The orb's speaking rings pulse with it. Rings on a timer would have looked exactly the same and meant nothing, which is the distinction the meter beside them exists to make.

setup is the newest, and it is the reason the sidecar now comes up even when it cannot answer anything. A first launch is precisely the launch on which the model has not been pulled — so preflight fails, and a sidecar that exits on that leaves the shell able to say only “the sidecar exited”, with the thing that would pull the model sitting on the other end of a socket that never opened. It listens anyway, says what is missing, and refuses turns until it is not. The block carries three facts rather than one because they are three different jobs: start Ollama — the only one this app cannot do for you — pull the model, download the voice models. preflight raises a single exception for the first two, which is right for a startup check and wrong for a screen somebody has to act on.

set_voice replaced three messages, and the three were the mistake. set_mode, set_mute and listen were separate controls for one fact, and no combination of them was useful: voice mode with the microphone muted is an ear with its stream stopped, and a live microphone in text mode hears you and answers in silence. One switch. On means the ear is open, the wake word is listening and replies are spoken; off means the window is a text box. Typing works either way.

Nothing announces the end of a turn on this socket any more, and that is deliberate. The sidecar used to broadcast state: idle when the token stream ended — which is while the synthesiser still has sentences queued, so the orb left speaking before the reply had been heard. The ear already knows when the room is quiet and already says listening then. So the socket says nothing, unless there is no ear to say it. Which gives idle a meaning it did not have: it is the closed microphone, and the only thing that means that.

Both ends of this list are checked by a test rather than by reading, because they are two files in two languages and a rename lands in one of them first. Every type the window sends must have a branch in the sidecar; every type the sidecar sends must have a case in the window; and a branch nothing reaches has to be named as deliberate or deleted. Writing it deleted one: setup had been accepted since the first run screen was built and nothing had ever sent it.

The ear runs on its own thread and reaches the socket through the loop rather than from that thread, which is the same rule as the turn worker and for the same reason.

The turn runs beside the read loop, never inside it

The obvious way to handle text_input is to await the turn where you read it. Do that and the connection reads nothing more until the turn ends — and with one client, which is every installation until the shell exists, that is every message sent mid-turn.

Both of the messages that matter mid-turn are then unreachable. The answer to a confirm sits unread in the socket buffer while the worker thread waits out its two-minute timeout and then refuses it, so every overwrite hangs and is declined. And cancel — the stop button, the whole of milestone 9 from the UI's side — is read only after the turn it was meant to stop has already committed.

So the turn is spawned as its own task and the read loop stays live. One-turn-at-a-time still holds: a second input arriving mid-turn meets the same lock it always did, and is answered with a notice.

This is a bug you cannot see by reading the handler. It was found by driving a real socket, and the tests that pin it drive one too — against the old code they take two minutes and fail, which is exactly what a person would have experienced.

A character, and what it costs

The first version of this prompt described a competent tool-caller, and that is exactly what it produced: “The file has been moved successfully.” Correct, and dead. It also treated a greeting as a work order, and it would cheerfully say it was about to do something and then not do it.

The fix is not a page of adjectives, and could not be — there is no room for one, and a model imitates the register it is handed far more reliably than it follows a description of one. Two lines do it: warm, quick and a little funny; teases lightly, never grovels; short sentences, interesting part first. Asked to move a file now, it says “Done. gold.txt is in the work folder now. Was it ready to be moved, or was I just being helpful?”

Two of the new lines are not character at all. “If you say you are about to do something, do it in the same turn” and “talking is a real thing to do” are the two failures the old prompt actually produced, written down as rules. The whole thing costs 95 tokens and moved overhead_tokens for the fifth time, which is the honest price and is why it is stated here rather than admired.

It talks before the work, because it cannot talk during it

A turn that runs four tools used to be four tools of silence and then an answer, and the longer the job the more it looked like a hang. The obvious fix is to pass on whatever the model says alongside its tool calls — the loop has always produced that and always thrown it away.

Measured, that content is empty every single time. With the system prompt asking for a line; with an extra system message ordering one immediately before the call. The chat template puts the tool calls where the message would be. So building on it would have produced a sink that never fires, which is indistinguishable from a broken one and is a mistake this document has already recorded twice.

The line is therefore generated on purpose: one call, no tools bound, forty tokens, before the first tool of a turn runs. About a third of a second against several seconds of silence, still in the model's own voice, and aimed at the longest gap — the one right after a person stops speaking, when nothing has happened yet. From a real turn: “I'm reading gold.txt right now.”

The backstage trace

Every other message on this socket reports an outcome. token is the answer, notice is a thing you are owed an apology for, done is the post-mortem. None of them answers the question a person actually has while they wait, which is whether it is stuck or reading — and, the one that forced this channel into existence: did it look in long-term memory at all, or did that quietly fail and nobody said?

That question has no answer anywhere else, because retrieval swallows every exception on purpose — memory must never be the reason a turn fails. The cost of that decision is that three very different outcomes look identical from outside: a store that is empty, a store that was searched and had nothing inside the distance cutoff, and a store that could not be opened at all. Only the last is a fault. So retrieval records which of the three it was, and the trace says so in those words:

The phrasing lives in its own module, sunday/trace.py, away from the runtime. Two reasons, and the second is the load-bearing one. The runtime should read as the machine it is. And these lines are meant to be read by someone tired at the end of a debugging session, so they are allowed to be funny — on one condition, written at the top of that file: numbers before jokes. A joke that costs you a number is a bug there, not a style choice. The lines are also deterministic, because two runs of the same turn that produce two different traces cannot be diffed.

It is on by default and meant to stay on until the shell ships. [ui] trace = false in config turns it off; SUNDAY_TRACE=0 overrides for a single run without a file edit.

The closing line goes before done, not after

Clients treat done as the end of the turn and stop listening. A summary emitted after it is a summary nobody reads — the same ordering mistake as emitting notices after the sinks were cleared, which is why no client ever saw a redaction.

Lifecycle

  • Start. Shell launches, spawns the sidecar, shows the orb in a connecting state until the first state event arrives.
  • Health. Ping every 5 s. Three missed pings means the sidecar is gone.
  • Crash. Restart it, up to three times in a minute. Beyond that, show the error and stop — a restart loop is worse than an honest failure.
  • Quit. Shell sends shutdown, the sidecar flushes the session summary to Chroma, then exits. Force-kill after 5 s. Closing the window is not quitting — it hides, because there is a tray icon and the thing is meant to be listening while you do something else. Quit lives in the tray menu and in the window's own control, and both take the same route: ask over the socket, wait, then stop waiting. A quit that killed the process first would lose the session summary every time.

Starting and stopping are two different clocks

vad_silence_ms decides when you have finished, and it may only run once you have begun. Conflate the two and the 700 ms stopwatch starts on the wake phrase sitting in the pre-roll — and the pause between the phrase and the question is routinely twice that. Measured here at 1.38 s, from someone who knew what they were about to ask. The clip then closes inside the pause, holding nothing but the tail of the trigger, which the cough guard correctly discards. From outside it is silence: the wake word visibly fires and nothing ever happens again.

So until you have begun, the only clock running is lead_in_ms, which abandons the clip entirely — the television, mostly. And “begun” is min_clip_ms of speech, not one window of it: a VAD window straddling the wake word carries 32 ms of the phrase across the boundary, and one window was enough to start the wrong clock. It is the same number as the cough guard on purpose. That advice outlived its own conclusion. The plan was hey_jarvis now and “hey sunday” later; what happened instead is that the assistant was renamed to Alexa, which is already one of the three. So the wake phrase is its name, nothing was trained, and the afternoon budgeted for it was not spent.

The turn, in audio terms

  1. Wake word fires. Orb pulses. The last 0.5 s of the ring buffer is kept, because people start talking before they finish the trigger. It goes through the VAD like everything else — half a second nobody looked at is half a second that can only be cut when the clip is trimmed.
  2. VAD marks speech. Recording continues while it stays true. Speech after the wake word: the pre-roll almost always holds the trigger phrase, and that is not the question.
  3. VAD sees 700 ms of silence — you are done. The clip closes.
  4. The transcriber turns the clip into text. Orb collapses inward.
  5. Text goes into the graph exactly as if you had typed it.

Two guards on step 3: a clip with less than 300 ms of speech in it is discarded as a cough, and one longer than 30 s is force-closed and transcribed anyway. The first is measured against the speech rather than the clip because every clip carries pre-roll and trailing silence, so a guard on the total could never fire.

Device changes

Unplugging a headset mid-session is normal and must not kill the app. The audio thread watches for stream errors, tears down, re-enumerates, and rebinds to the configured device or the system default. The orb shows an error state for the second or two this takes.

Desktop 03

Echo control

The assistant speaks through your speakers and listens through your microphone. Without care it hears itself, transcribes its own reply, and answers it. On a laptop with open speakers this happens immediately.

Three layers, because no single one is enough.

Layer 1 — acoustic echo cancellation

Feed the AEC two signals: the microphone, and the exact PCM buffer you handed to the output stream. It subtracts the second from the first.

This is the ideal case. Most echo cancellers have to guess what came out of the speaker; here you generated it, so the reference is perfect. What remains is room reflection and speaker distortion, which is what AEC is actually good at.

Use speexdsp or webrtc-audio-processing. Both need the reference delay-aligned with the capture — measure it once on your hardware and store it in config.

Layer 2 — the self-speech gate

While Kokoro is playing, raise the VAD threshold and require a sustained burst rather than a single frame. Residual echo is quiet and choppy; a person interrupting is loud and continuous.

“While playing” outlasts the last write by tail_ms. write returns when the sound card has accepted a block, not when it has played it, so at the last one there is a buffer still to come and then a room still ringing. Three defences key off that single flag — the raised bar, the barge-in floor, and the transcript check, which may only examine clips recorded while it is set — and all three switched off together while the voice was still audible. The follow-up window then opened into exactly that.

And a floor computed from what is playing needs to know what is playing. The reference lags the first block of a reply by aec_delay_ms, so it is zero at the start of every sentence — and a fraction of zero is no floor at all, at the moment the room is loudest. Not knowing closes the gate now rather than opening it: the measurement is zero and there is no measurement are different facts.

This is also the barge-in detector. When it fires: stop playback, flush the TTS queue, cancel the model stream, drop the partial turn, open a new one. Target under 100 ms from your first syllable to silence.

Layer 3 — the transcript check

Whatever slips through the first two layers still has to survive this. Compare the fresh transcript against the sentence Kokoro is currently speaking — normalise both, take token overlap, and discard above 0.6 similarity.

“Currently” is load-bearing, and widening it breaks the check completely. Built as a comparison against the last few things said, whether or not anything was playing, it throws away the questions it exists to protect — because a reply always contains its question’s subject, so the next question about that subject overlaps the answer. Measured against a real reply: “what is the weather in Jakarta” scores 0.67, and the follow-up “and the humidity” scores 1.00, every word of it being in the answer. No cutoff saves that; saving the second means raising the bar past 1.0. So the clip carries a flag saying whether any of it was recorded while Sunday was talking, and a clip recorded into a silent room is never tested at all.

It is a handful of lines and it catches the case where the room is echoey and the AEC was not quite enough. Cheap insurance for the most annoying possible bug — as long as it is only ever asked about audio that could contain an echo.

The overlap is asymmetric on purpose: it asks how much of what was heard was already being said, because a microphone catches part of a sentence far more often than all of it. That is the right property, and it is also what makes a short follow-up score 1.00 — which is why the speaking test has to come first.

And the trivial answer

Headphones make all three layers unnecessary. Build them anyway — you will not always be wearing headphones — but test with headphones first so you can tell an echo bug apart from a pipeline bug.

Desktop 04

The orb

A web of particles in the middle of the window. It is not decoration — it is the only status display, and it has to answer one question at a glance: what is it doing right now?

Every state event from the sidecar maps to one look. Teal and red mean in the window what they mean in this document, which is what lets the orb answer a second question too: is anything leaving my machine? The document’s amber does not appear in the window at all — see below.

It was a single glowing sphere first, and a sphere has two channels: how bright it is and what colour it is. A web has a third — how connected it is — and that turns out to be the one that carries the states. transcribing knits the mesh shut, thinking shakes the nodes off their lattice points, idle drops most of the links and leaves dust drifting, and speaking sends a wave front-to-back through it that moves with what is reaching the sound card. Thirty-four points, rotated on two axes, every pair measured each frame: 561 distance checks, which is less work than one of the three radial gradients the sphere drew.

White, and that is a privacy decision rather than a taste one. Colour is spent on two facts and nothing else: teal while something is reaching off this machine, red for a refusal. Everything the assistant does on your own machine — thinking, listening, speaking, running a local tool — is white.

It went three, then two. Amber first covered thinking, speaking and local tools, so teal arriving was a shift between two warm states; cutting it to local tools alone helped, and cutting it entirely helped again. The orb is white until it is not, and the moment it is not is the moment that matters. Local work is told apart from thinking by the two channels a mesh has and a sphere did not: it is denser (0.98 against 0.86) and slower (0.7 against 1.15).

A state in this table is a promise that something emits it

Two of the rows above were written here and produced nowhere, and stayed that way through voice being built and finished. The ear turned a wake event straight into listening, so the flash never happened; and nothing carried an output amplitude, so the rings had nothing to pulse with. From outside, a state that never arrives is indistinguishable from a state that is broken — which is the same observation the backstage trace exists to make about memory.

So the two sets are compared by a test rather than by reading. It takes the states the orb can draw from the renderer, takes the states the package can emit from the Python, and requires each to cover the other — with the three that are deliberately derived from a tool or blocked message, and the one the shell owns before the socket has said anything, named as the exceptions they are. Writing that test found a third thing immediately: it read one of the three spellings the package uses to emit a state, and so reported thinking — which happens on every turn that thinks — as having no producer at all.

Rendering budget

  • Canvas 2D, no library. A lattice of points, the lines between the near ones, and one radial gradient behind them. A few hundred lines.
  • 60 fps focused, 10 fps blurred, paused when hidden. Driven by requestAnimationFrame plus the visibility API.
  • Respect prefers-reduced-motion. States still change colour; they stop moving.
  • Never WebGL. Worth repeating: that GPU is holding your models.
  • Everything absolute is scaled off the size. The node radii and the link width were tuned at a 190px orb; the orb is a fraction of its panel now — min(300px, 84%) — so they ride unit / 95, bounded. Without that the same web is chunky in the compact window and wispy in a wide one, which is what a drawing does when it keeps one hard-coded size and its box stops having one.
  • Assigning canvas.width wipes the bitmap even when the number has not changed, and a ResizeObserver notifies as soon as it starts observing — so an unguarded resize throws away the frame just drawn. Invisible sixty times a second; a blank orb once.
  • Clear in device pixels, not CSS ones. canvas.width is round(w × dpr) and the transform is dpr; at 1.25 those differ by half a pixel, so clearing the CSS box leaves the buffer’s last column and row untouched and an early frame’s ink stays there for the life of the window. It reads as a hairline beside the orb, and four plausible stylesheet causes all explain it. What separated them: capture the window twice and diff. The orb changed 7138 pixels between frames; the line changed none. A still line beside a moving drawing is not being drawn, it is being left. A ResizeObserver sits beside the fix, because a window resize is not the only thing that changes this element.

The rest of the window

  • Three columns, 3:4:3, on one ground. The orb has the left wall to itself — a fraction of that wall rather than a fixed square, centred in it as a group with its name and its two controls — the conversation is the middle, the backstage is the right. All three sit on the same background with a hairline between them — the rule that must not exist is one down the side of the orb, which is a thing made of light; a rule between two panels is what says where each begins. The widths are fractions (minmax(0, 3fr) and so on, because a grid track’s floor is otherwise its content) rather than fixed pixels: with fixed side panels the middle column absorbs every pixel of a wider window and has no use for them, being capped at a 720px reading measure already.
  • The backstage is always open: a panel that answers “is it stuck, or is it reading?” is no use to somebody who has to decide to open it before the question occurs to them. It keeps the last three questions rather than only the one in flight, with a rule and 30px between them — a turn boundary is not an act boundary, and 5/5 filing running straight into the next 0/5 wake is the one break the panel was not drawing.
  • Input row: a text box that is always usable, and one button that sends or stops. The box wraps rather than scrolling — it grows to about seven lines and scrolls only past that — because an input answers a long question by hiding the beginning of it, and the questions this gets asked are frequently a path plus a sentence. Return sends; Shift+Return makes a line. There is no mic button, because there is nothing for it to do — the microphone is either open or it is not, and the wake word is how you address it.
  • One voice switch, and it is a glyph rather than a sentence. On means the ear is open, the wake word is listening and replies are spoken; off means this is a text box, and the microphone wears a slash. Typing works either way, which is exactly why it is a switch and not a mode. It sits beside the compact control, two circles under the orb: the line under the orb already says listening or microphone off, so a labelled button beneath it said the same thing twice and took the wall the orb was given on purpose.
  • Notices appear as one quiet line in the transcript, where they happened: redactions, blocked lookups, tool errors.
  • What it is doing, where you are looking. While a turn is in flight the newest backstage line also sits directly above the composer, with one mote orbiting beside it. Deliberately the same text rather than a second wording, and a mote rather than a bar, because a bar promises a proportion nothing here can measure.
  • A way back down. The transcript follows the bottom only while you are already there — yanking somebody back mid-answer is the one thing a transcript can do that makes it unreadable. A Newest button floats over it exactly while there is somewhere to go, which is the other half of that rule and was missing.
  • Quitting is a word. exit, quit or bye alone on a line closes the app, in the right order — shutdown over the socket first, because that is what flushes the session summary into Chroma. It is handled in the window rather than as a tool: quitting is something the window does, not something the model may decide to do.
  • Compact mode: a small always-on-top window showing just the orb, for when it is listening in the background. It runs at the focused frame rate in both states — fps_blurred is for a window nobody is looking at, and this is the one window looked at more when it is blurred than when it is not, so throttling it to ten frames a second was visible as exactly what it was. It is the same window resized, undecorated and pinned — a second window would be two orbs, two sockets and two things to keep in step, for a feature whose entire content is “the same orb, smaller”.
    It has three ways out, and one of them is not in the window. A double click anywhere expands it, a button in the corner expands it, and the tray menu can pull it back. The first version had one way out, on an element that also carried a CSS drag region — and a drag region swallows mouse events before the page sees them, so the click could never fire and the app was a small circle, always on top, with no menu and no title bar. A mode that can make the window hard to click needs an escape that does not depend on clicking the window.
    Which is also why the whole window is a handle without being a drag region: mousedown reaches the page, the page reads event.detail, and either expands or asks the shell for start_dragging(). A CSS drag region cannot do both, because it never lets the page find out that a second click happened.

Part three — reference

Resources

Sized to live on a single 6 GB card with everything loaded at once. Nothing sleeps, nothing swaps, nothing has to wake up. That is deliberate — the pipeline is fast partly because there is no load schedule to get wrong, and barge-in is instant because the transcriber is already there.

What the voice models actually cost the card: nothing

This section was written expecting Moonshine and Kokoro on the GPU. They are not. The onnxruntime build that comes with the rest of the dependencies offers CPUExecutionProvider and nothing else, so all four voice models — wake word, VAD, Moonshine, Kokoro — run on the processor and hold 0 GB of VRAM. They cost about 1.2 GB of ordinary RAM in the sidecar process, and roughly a fifth of one core while listening.

Measured with everything running: qwen3.5:4b at a 23552 window is 4724 MiB, entirely on the card, and the whole machine still fits inside 6144 with a browser open — 1279 MiB to spare, which is the headroom that keeps it there when something else wants the GPU. So the budget below is a plan for a build that would put the synthesiser on the GPU, and the build that exists has considerably more room than it. Two of the three savings underneath are consequently free money that is not needed: there is one CUDA context, not three, and the desktop is no longer competing with a full card.

How this fails, and why it is sneaky

Running out does not crash anything. Ollama quietly moves layers to the CPU and keeps going — several times slower. It reads as “the model got dumb” when it actually ran out of room.

Check with ollama ps. It should say 100% GPU. Anything else means you are over, and no amount of prompt tuning will fix it.

  • Ollama cannot see the others. It sizes its allocation from what looks free when it loads, with no idea Moonshine, Kokoro or WebView2 exist. Start Ollama first and let the rest fit around it, or cap its budget explicitly.
  • Measure with the app open and a browser running. A clean benchmark on an idle machine will tell you it fits. That is not the condition it lives in.
  • The 2b is the only thing you can grow. Everything else is fixed. If you later want a 4b, you need the iGPU move and probably a 8 GB card.

What the thirteen built milestones actually cost

Most of the work was writing down what this document already said. Six things were not: the splitter's abbreviation heuristic, the silent withdrawal of the door, retrieval handing the model its own prose back, the limit on the airlock's guarantee, Moonshine's refusal to transcribe a clip padded with silence, and the two clocks above. Every one was found by running it, not by reading it — and none would have been caught by a test written from the spec, because the spec was the thing that was wrong.

Voice sharpened that. Its four faults all presented as the same blank terminal, and three of the four were downstream of components that were working perfectly. The tool that ended it was not a test but sunday.audio.check, which measures the four pieces against your own voice and keeps the recording — because the room and the microphone are variables the suite cannot see, and every threshold here is a guess until somebody measures one.

That is the argument for keeping the terminal client alive the whole way, and for the browser page at milestone 10. Both exist to let you watch a real turn happen before there is a window in the way — and the window, when it came, was built against a running sidecar in an ordinary browser tab for exactly the same reason. A reload is a tenth of a second; a Rust build is not.

The window found two more of the same kind, and they are the kind this section is about. wake and the speaking amplitude were both in the state table from the first draft of this document, and neither had anything on the other end — the ear turned a wake event straight into listening, and the level event carried the microphone alone. Reading the specification would never have shown that. Building the thing it specifies is what shows it.

And when one of those runs produces a rule, the test is written from the rule and not from the run. This has now cost the same thing twice. “A fast path never fires on a compound message” was tested against five real transcripts, none of which happened to be a capability question, so the one unguarded path of the three passed every run for a milestone. “Every refusal tells the model what to say” was tested against the four refusals that had been reported, and the one it left out was the credential source — the single refusal in the sandbox that exists because taint cannot undo a laundered copy after the fact. Both suites were green throughout. A rule that says never is tested by enumerating the paths it covers; the bug reports are where you learned the rule, not what you check.

Open decisions

  • How much of the window memory should take. The window is no longer the binding constraint, so the five slices are an open question rather than a forced one: more recent turns against slower prompt processing and a 2b whose attention is already thin. They are left where they were on purpose — the window being wrong was a fact, the slices being right is a judgement.
  • Tool-call reliability. Benchmark properly: twenty real phrasings, count how often the right tool is picked with the right arguments. Everything here rests on that number. Informally it has held up across the build; that is not the same as measured.
  • Model size. Closed. The 2b slipped person and decorated; qwen3.5:4b fixed both, kept the tool accuracy the 2b had been chosen for, and cost a second a turn and a window brought down to 23552 to stay on the card. qwen3:8b is pulled and would need the window smaller again — that comparison is open, and the iGPU move in Resources is no longer a precondition for it.
  • Retrieval distance. The 0.45 default is still a guess, but it is now a guess in cosine space with every lookup's score logged, so it can be set from data rather than argued about.
  • AEC delay. Hardware-specific. Measure it once with a click test and write it into config.
  • Wake phrase. Closed. The assistant is called Alexa and alexa ships pretrained, so the phrase is its own name and no model had to be trained. The threshold is 0.3, measured against hey_jarvis — worth re-measuring for the new phrase with python -m sunday.audio.check, since it is a property of the phrase and the voice saying it. It lives in [wake] threshold and nowhere else: the 0.5 it started at survived in a default argument long after it was measured away, which is a wrong number that stays unreachable until somebody writes the call site that omits the keyword.
  • Search provider. DuckDuckGo needs no key and has no quota to manage. Result quality has been adequate through the build, including on questions the snippets alone could not answer. Brave Search API is still the drop-in upgrade.
  • Showing outgoing queries. Settled for the debug client, open for the shipped one. The socket emits a query event carrying the exact text that left, and watching it appear is the single most reassuring thing in the whole build. Whether the real window shows it, and what it does in voice, is still a question.
  • Session boundary. Ten minutes idle is a guess. If you talk to it in bursts all day, you may want longer.
  • Token counting. New. The slices are enforced against a 3.6-characters-per-token estimate rather than a real tokeniser, to keep a network call out of the context accounting. If the budget ever proves too loose or too tight, ask Ollama to count.

Deliberately out of scope

  • Non-English speech. Both transcribers are English-only — Parakeet as much as Moonshine. Multilingual means a bigger STT and a different memory budget, and it is RAM rather than VRAM, because the voice models are on the processor.
  • Streaming STT. Would remove the wait-until-you-stop delay, at the cost of a larger model.
  • Entropy and Luhn detection in the guardrail — see Stage 05 for why.
  • Writing to calendar or mail. Read-only scopes only. An assistant that can send mail is a different risk conversation. Files are the one thing it may write, and replacing one asks first — see Stage 04.
  • Vision. Nothing here needs it, though a multimodal local model would let you hand it a screenshot without adding a component.
  • Multi-user or networked access. Loopback only, one machine, one person.
